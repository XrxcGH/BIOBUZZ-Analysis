### 1.6 League teams: the meets are not warm-ups

**[FACT]** V0 §14 (final text): a League requires a minimum of **10 League Meet matches** per team; League Meets play 5-6 qualification matches each with **no playoffs and no judging**. **League Tournament rankings are computed using each team's top 10 League Meet matches plus the League Tournament matches.** Teams who played fewer than 10 League Meet matches get effectively-zero ranking points for the missing ones.

**Consequence:** if you are in a League, **League Meet 1 (earliest Oct 19, 2026) carries permanent ranking weight.** A league team must hit "reliable and scoring" by **Week 5**, not Week 9. That is the single biggest calendar difference between league and qualifier regions, and it is the first thing to confirm with your Program Delivery Partner.

### 1.7 Find YOUR dates — and the three-event rule that governs them

#### 1.7.1 The rule most small teams learn too late

**[FACT]** V0 §4 Advancement: *"Teams may participate in more than three entry-level events but are eligible to advance from the first three chronological events."* Entry-level events are **Qualifying Tournaments (QT)** and **League Tournaments (LT)** (Figure 4-2). Above them: optional Super Qualifying Tournament (SQT, one per team maximum), then Regional Championship (RCMP), then FIRST Championship / FIRST Premier Event.

**[FACT]** V0 §4: *"Teams are only eligible to advance from events within their HOME REGION."* Out-of-region invitational play is legal but never advancement-eligible. Your HOME REGION is shown on your FTC-Events team page. **[FACT]** Teams in regions with no local Program Delivery Partner, or *"geographically isolated within their HOME REGION,"* can be reassigned to a more accessible region **once per season** by emailing `customerservice@firstinspires.org`.

**[FACT]** V0 §4: *"Teams may only participate in one League per season."* **[FACT]** The local PDP sets advancement numbers from every tournament up to the RCMP; FIRST staff set RCMP → FCMP/FPE advancement.

**Cadence consequence [JUDGMENT] — this is the sharpest scheduling constraint in the document.** Three chronological shots. A small team's instinct is to sign up for the earliest possible qualifier "for the experience." Under this rule that shakedown event **consumes one of your three advancement chances** with a robot you know is not ready. Two disciplined responses:

| Situation | Do |
|---|---|
| You want match experience before you are ready | Attend a **scrimmage / preview / off-season event** instead. Scrimmages are neither QTs nor LTs, so they do not consume one of the three. [JUDGMENT, grounded in §4's definition of entry-level events — confirm with your PDP before registering.] |
| Your region only runs 2-3 qualifiers total | Then every one counts; G6 (competition ready) is not negotiable, and you should pick the **latest** qualifier your region offers to buy build weeks. |
| You are in a League | The three-event rule barely applies — the League Tournament is your one entry-level event and League Meets feed its ranking (§1.6). Readiness must come ~4 weeks earlier. |

## 2. The canonical elite-team season timeline

### 2.0 The official baseline — and how far above it the elite actually sit

**[FACT]** FIRST publishes its own expected time commitment in the **FIRST Tech Challenge Mentor Manual** (<https://info.firstinspires.org/hubfs/web/program/ftc/ftc-mentor-manual.pdf>, read 2026-08-22; the copy served today is stamped *"2024-2025 Season Specific Information"* — the numbers are program guidance, not season-specific rules):

> *"we expect students to spend at a minimum **26 hours per season**... meeting approximately two times a week, for 1-1.5 hours each meeting, for 9-12 weeks. An average student would spend approximately **37 hours** total per season... At the more intense end students could spend up to **12 hours per week, over a course of 15 weeks, for a total of 180 hours**."*

**[FACT]** Same document, more guidance worth arguing with:

- *"Sessions lasting 1-2 hours are the most productive."*
- *"FIRST Tech Challenge recommends starting with two shorter meetings, or one longer meeting per week during the teambuilding stage."*
- *"During the design and build phase, meetings should take place more frequently... Depending on the role assignments, not every team member needs to be at every meeting."*
- *"If an event is scheduled more than eight weeks from the Kickoff date, a less intensive schedule can be created."*
- The manual's **Season Timeline** chart runs May → April with seven bands: *Registration Opens, Pre-Season, Kickoff, Build Season, Qualifying Events, Regional Championships, FIRST Championship.* Note that **"Pre-Season" is a named, official phase** — §3 is not an invention.

**[JUDGMENT] Put the three numbers next to each other and the whole document snaps into focus:**

| Level | Student-hours per season | Who |
|---|---|---|
| FIRST's stated **minimum** | **26 h** | The floor for "participated" |
| FIRST's stated **average** | **37 h** | A typical rec-league team |
| FIRST's stated **intense end** | **180 h** (12 h/wk × 15 wk) | What FIRST considers a heavy commitment |
| **This plan (§10.4)** | **~115 h to event 1**, ~250-300 h to April | 12 h/wk of meetings + 3 h/wk async |
| **Elite FTC** | **~500+ h** | 20 h/wk in person (FTC 23513, §2.3), plus a summer training programme |

**Three consequences [JUDGMENT]:**

1. **You are already planning to be at the top of FIRST's own scale.** The plan in this document is *above* the "intense end" that FIRST describes. That is the right ambition for the deliverables you want, but say it out loud to students and parents in Week A, in hours, before anyone commits. A team that discovers the real number in November loses people in November.
2. **The elite gap is not a work-ethic gap, it is a headcount-and-summer gap.** 20 h/week × 20 students is unreachable by trying harder. It is reachable only by §3.5 (bootcamp), §7.2 (off-season) and §8.7 (async). Read those three as the actual competitive response.
3. **Argue with the "1-2 hours is most productive" line, and then respect half of it.** [JUDGMENT] FIRST is right that *attention* decays; the §8.2 agenda answers it not by shortening the meeting but by **breaking three hours into blocks of 10 / 120 / 20 / 12 / 8 minutes with different modes**. What you must not do is run an undifferentiated 3-hour work session and expect hour three to produce anything. The block structure *is* the compliance with that guidance.

### 2.1 Phase table

Weeks are numbered from kickoff. W0 = Sep 12-13, 2026.

| # | Phase | Dates | Duration | Exit gate (the decision that ends it) | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### 2.2 Phase gates in detail

Gates are **binary and dated**. "We're mostly there" is a failed gate. [JUDGMENT] Write each gate date on a whiteboard on kickoff day and do not move it — move *scope* instead.

| Gate | Date | Pass criteria | If you fail it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **G0 — Pre-season ready** | Sep 11 | Chassis drives a figure-8 by gamepad; `git push` works; a field exists; Pollen in hand | Kickoff weekend becomes a build-the-drivetrain weekend and you lose the strategy window |
| **G1 — Archetype chosen** | Sun Sep 13, end of day | One paragraph everyone can recite: *"We are a ___ robot that does ___ and ___, and does not do ___"* | You prototype in five directions and freeze nothing |
| **G2 — Strategy frozen** | Sun Sep 20 | Ranked scoring-task list with points-per-second math; explicit "not doing" list | Scope creep to the end of the season |
| **G3 — Mechanisms selected** | Sun Oct 4 | Each mechanism: one concept, one page of test numbers | CAD stalls waiting on prototypes |
| **G4 — DESIGN FREEZE** | Sun Oct 18 | Assembly CAD complete; BOM ordered; nothing new may be added | Parts arrive after you needed them; integration never converges |
| **G5 — Robot alive** | Sun Nov 1 | Drives + every mechanism actuates + passes your own inspection checklist | Programming has nothing to run on; driver practice never happens |
| **G6 — Competition ready** | Sun Nov 8 | Auto ≥8/10; 6 consecutive full matches with no failure; portfolio printed; scouting sheets printed | You debug in the pit instead of competing |
| **G7 — Dress rehearsal** | Thu Nov 12 | Full simulated event day: check-in, inspection, 6 matches, judge interview | Preventable event-day surprises |

### 2.3 Where the elite-team pattern comes from

**[FACT]** FRC 254's published Day 1 log for the 2014 season is the canonical elite kickoff record: local kickoff broadcast in the evening, team reconvened at 10:00 p.m., ~70 students in the lab for systematic manual review, a mentor-built **spreadsheet scoring model** taking point values × expected action duration × success probability to produce weighted point expectations, and ~20 students prototyping **two competing shooter concepts** after dinner, lab closing at 11:00 p.m. (<https://www.team254.com/2014frc-day1/>). The FTC version of this compresses to a smaller crew but keeps the three artifacts: **read the manual as a team, build a scoring model, prototype the hard mechanism the same day.**

**[FACT]** FTC-native evidence of the same cadence, from Open Alliance build threads on Chief Delphi:

- **FTC 23513 I.N.T. Robotics** (New York City, third-year community team, 2025 Rookie All-Star winners) stated their season plan explicitly: *"We plan to meet roughly 20 hours per week in person, maintain a tight prototype cadence, and run routine progress checks on CAD and software."* They co-hosted a **"Robot in 30 Hours"** kickoff event, and their **Week 1-2 post (Sep 21, 2025 — 15 days after the Sep 6 kickoff)** already contained: a working rubber-band intake prototype built during kickoff, an indexer concept with two sketched operating states, a prototyped dual-wheel shooter, a climber concept with **FEA already run on the linkage bars**, a swerve chassis layout, and a complete written state machine for the game-piece-sorting code. Later posts nail down real numbers: 4 in. flywheel, 1620 RPM motor at 2:1 → 3240 RPM, 3 mm compression, 4.5 in. hood gap. (<https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/ftc-23513-i-n-t-robotics-open-alliance-build-thread-2025-2026-season/506299>)
- **FTC 23619 Overture** (Monterrey, Mexico; FTC arm of FRC 7421) documents the iterate-between-events loop with a rigorous **failure → fix** format per event: odometry drift, a bent 3/8 in. 7075-T6 intake shaft, sunlight washing out AprilTags on an Arducam at an outdoor venue, a burned Expansion Hub, a broken 3D-printed number holder. Their between-event changes were concrete: swap Arducam → Limelight 3A, shooting angle 40° → 50°, add 3D-printed flywheels for inertia, sushi rollers → 2 in. Stealth wheels, relocate the odometry pod away from motor magnetic fields, retire the unused climber to save weight. They also built and beta-tested a **two-part scouting app** (React/Vite capture → QR code → Streamlit processing) with a stated workflow: *"The day before the event, we set a cutoff for any changes to data points and scouting assignments, publish the schedule, and begin data collection."* (<https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/overture-23619-ftc-build-blog-2025-2026-open-alliance/508083>)

**[FACT]** The **FTC Open Alliance** (<https://theopenalliance.org/ftc>) is the mechanism that makes this visible: member teams publish CAD, code, media and a Chief Delphi build thread, and *"should share both successful progress and frustrating challenges."* Registration for the 2025-26 season opened at 10:00 a.m. on kickoff morning. **Submissions for the 2025-26 season are currently closed; the site states registration reopens at Kickoff.**

**[JUDGMENT]** For a small team, joining the FTC Open Alliance on kickoff day is one of the highest return-on-effort decisions available. It costs nothing, it forces the documentation discipline the Think and Control awards require, and it converts your build log into portfolio material and Connect-award evidence as a by-product of work you were doing anyway.

### 2.4 Two more reference points: the shift model and the practice-match benchmark

**[FACT] How a 30-hour kickoff build is actually staffed.** FTC teams Overcharged and RevAmped published the operating model for their Robot in 30 Hours (<https://overchargedrobotics.org/ri30hr/>, Rover Ruckus season — an older season, but the structure is the transferable part):

- **26 members across two teams**, 13 each.
- **Three eight-hour shifts** (24 h), each shift *"prototyping a section of the robot, finalizing a component, making a CAD model of the impromptu robot, wiring everything up, or documenting all the events that pass by."*
- The remaining six hours were split: *"the first three are designated for brainstorming"* and *"the last three for an 'all hands on deck' section."*
- Preparation was explicit: *"We've spent the entire summer training our members to get ready for this challenge,"* including **two weeks in August of an intensive training course** covering building, CAD, programming and electronics, so that all seven new members could contribute. Veterans built a practice chassis over the summer.

**[JUDGMENT]** Two things transfer to a 5-10 student team even though 26 people do not. First, **the capability that makes a big kickoff push possible is bought in August, not in September** — see §3.5. Second, **shifts beat heroics**: 3 h brainstorm → rotating work blocks → all-hands finish is a better shape than one undifferentiated 14-hour Saturday, and it is the shape §4 already uses. If you attempt a long build weekend, roster it in shifts and let people sleep.

**[FACT] The practice-match benchmark.** GM0 (<https://gm0.org/en/latest/docs/design-skills/design-strategy.html>) states plainly: *"A persistent problem with new teams is neglecting driver practice. Drive practice is to be done throughout the season, **not the week before competition.**"* And it gives the elite yardstick: *"By April's world championships, most top teams have run hundreds of practice matches."* It also sets a realistic target for a newer team: *"Typically, teams which have a solid autonomous and consistent endgame can be competitive at the Qualifier level. This is a recommended goal for new teams."*

**[JUDGMENT]** Convert that into a countable target on your gate board. "Hundreds of practice matches" by late April, from a robot alive on Nov 1, is roughly **25 weeks × 8-12 full matches/week**. Two full matches per meeting, three meetings a week, is exactly that rate — which is why §8.2 makes a **20-minute field block mandatory at every meeting**. The benchmark is not reachable by practising harder in April; it is only reachable by practising every week from November.

**[FACT] Competitive prototyping — the method for §4's prototype block.** GM0 (<https://gm0.org/en/latest/docs/being-a-team/collaboration-and-efficiency.html>): *"2-3 designers pick a mechanism and develop their own take on the design, then physically prototype them to test against certain criteria. The most successful design integrates the most successful components of each and is iteratively improved by a singular designer."* GM0 illustrates it with **7149 Enforcers** in the PowerPlay season, where two designers each built an intake and the final design took the best of both.

**[JUDGMENT]** For a small team the value is in the last clause: **compete on concepts, then hand the winner to one person.** Two students in parallel for four hours, then one owner for three weeks. Parallel *ownership* of one mechanism is where small teams lose weeks.

### 2.5 When you miss a gate — the recovery playbook

**[JUDGMENT]** You will miss at least one gate. The failure is not missing it; the failure is discovering it late and then silently moving every downstream date. Each gate has exactly one legitimate recovery: **cut scope, hold the date.** Here is what that means concretely.

| Missed gate | Do **not** | Recovery move | What you pay | Point of no return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **G0** pre-season ready (Sep 11) | Spend kickoff weekend building a drivetrain | Buy/borrow a running chassis, or build a StarterBot Base in kickoff week evenings. Run §4 anyway — the strategy work does not need a robot. | Kickoff-week evenings | If the chassis is not running by **Sep 20**, drop to a single-mechanism archetype |
| **G1** archetype (Sep 13) | Keep prototyping in four directions | Force the decision at Monday's meeting with the data you have. An 80%-confident archetype on Monday beats a 95%-confident one in October. | Some option value | **Monday Sep 14.** Never later. |
| **G2** strategy frozen (Sep 20) | Re-open the archetype | Freeze the *"not doing"* list first — it is the half that actually saves time — and let the "doing" list stay ranked but unfinished. | Precision | Sep 27; after that, prototyping has no target |
| **G3** mechanisms selected (Oct 4) | Extend prototyping "one more week" | Select the **simplest** surviving concept for the mechanism that is behind, not the best. Log the discarded concepts — that is portfolio content and Think-award evidence. | Scoring ceiling | Oct 11. Past this, CAD cannot finish before parts lead times bite. |
| **G4** DESIGN FREEZE (Oct 18) | Slip the freeze | Freeze the robot **minus** the late mechanism, and treat that mechanism as a **bolt-on upgrade** with its own later gate. The robot must be able to compete without it. | One capability at event 1 | **This is the hardest gate to recover.** Slipping G4 by two weeks costs G5, G6 and all driver practice. |
| **G5** robot alive (Nov 1) | Keep adding mechanisms | Strip to drivetrain + one scoring mechanism, get *that* alive, wire it, and hand it to programming and the drivers. Bolt the rest on later or not at all. | Points | Nov 8. A robot not driving on Nov 8 will not be tuned for a Nov 14 event. |
| **G6** competition ready (Nov 8) | Cancel the dress rehearsal | Attend the event anyway, with reduced goals: pass inspection, complete every match, collect a failure list, do the judge interview. **[FACT]** V0 §6.1 — teams *"are eligible for awards even if they are attending the event without a ROBOT."* | Ranking at event 1 | None — attending is still correct |
| **G7** dress rehearsal (Nov 12) | Skip it | Run the 90-minute version: self-inspection + 3 back-to-back matches + pit-kit inventory. Never skip inspection and the pit kit. | Polish | None — the short version keeps most of the value |

**[JUDGMENT] The meta-rule:** when you slip, write on the gate board *what you cut*, not *what you moved*. A board that shows moved dates teaches the team that dates are negotiable. A board that shows cut scope teaches them that dates are not.

### 2.6 Case study — one real team's first eight weeks

**[FACT]** FTC **12736 Electric Mayhem Green**, Buffalo NY, from their public Open Alliance build thread (<https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/12736-electric-mayhem-green-2025-26-build-thread/506022>), DECODE season, kickoff Sep 6, 2025. Self-described: *"our 13th year as a FIRST program, and 9th year in FTC,"* a school-based team with sister team 12737, sharing a lab and meeting **three times a week after school**.

| Date | Week | What actually happened |
|---|---|---|
| Summer 2025 | — | Two summer camps: their **4th annual FLL camp** and **9th annual FTC camp**. In the FTC camp, *"four groups build FTC robots that would play this year's VEX game,"* finishing with *"a full tournament of the VEX game on the last day"* — used explicitly *"to onboard new students."* Lab investments: more storage, a permanent VEX field, expanded branding space, a deep clean. |
| **Sep 6** | W0 | Hosted local teams at their school for kickoff. Watched the reveal, then *"a field tour and a deconstruction of the rule book,"* then game analysis. New this year: a parent who is a creativity expert ran **divergent then convergent thinking** — *"you spend some time diverging, or getting as many ideas on the table as possible, then converging."* Also ran a Robot in 30 Hours build; by the end of it, *"WE ARE SHOOTING!"* |
| Sep 15 | W1 | **Simplified twice.** Replaced a moving intake with a **rubber-band intake stolen from their own VEX camp robot**; replaced a multi-shaft belted indexer with a rubber-band conveyor. Result: *"simpler, smaller and lighter."* Produced a 2D bot sketch in a public CAD document. |
| Sep 21 | W2 | Shooter prototype *"we built in an hour with a broken router"* — mounted on **an existing mecanum drivebase** to get driving-and-shooting immediately. |
| **Oct 6** | W4 | Combined W3+W4 recap; part of the lab ceiling had collapsed and closed the space. Intake design landed — and then **the critical flaw: the intake was too long for the turret to be mounted without hanging outside the 18 in. × 18 in. box.** The shooter could not shrink, so the intake was **halved in length** and widened to nearly full robot width. |
| Oct 8 – 22 | W5-W6 | **The router saga.** Chuck, then spindle motor, then the control system (*"aluminum got into the computer and fried the whole thing"*). A **city grant** for a replacement was secured but the funds were *"not available to us for another couple weeks."* Decision: *"The plan is to build the robot out of wood until the new router comes in. Then we will hopefully be able to quickly rebuild it out of polycarb for our first competition."* |
| **Oct 28** | ~W7.5 | *"The drivetrain, done. Intake, attached. Handoff, donezo... The best part? It's working."* Noted a brutal assembly-order dependency chain — the drivetrain could not be finished until the kickstand was on, the shooter could not go on until the intake was in. And the self-comparison: *"I like to think back to where we were this time last year... the CAD was just barely/not done. So major improvements."* |

**[JUDGMENT] Five things this validates or teaches:**

1. **The G5 (robot alive) date is right, and it is ambitious.** A 9th-year FTC team meeting three times a week got a working robot at **W7.5**. §2.2 puts G5 at **W7**. Treat G5 as a stretch target that good teams hit, not as a comfortable milestone — and note that this team hit it *a year earlier in relative terms than they had the season before*, which is what an experienced program buys you.
2. **The 18-inch cube kills a mechanism in Week 4, not at inspection.** Their intake was designed, then discovered to be un-mountable. Add an explicit envelope check — R102's 18 in. cube and R105's expansion limits — to **every** CAD review, not just the freeze review (§8.4).
3. **Tooling is a single point of failure and nobody plans for it.** Their most-used machine died mid-build. **[JUDGMENT]** Write down your fallback manufacturing path *before* you need it: if the 3D printer / router / laser cutter dies, what is plan B, and who owns one? For a small team the answer is usually a drill press, a band saw, a printed template and plywood — which is exactly what this team fell back on.
4. **Material substitution is legitimate schedule insurance.** Building the whole robot in wood to keep integration and programming moving, with a polycarb rebuild planned before competition, is cheap, legal under R304, and keeps the critical path alive. **[JUDGMENT]** For a lean team this generalises: *build the geometry in the cheap material first.* Plywood and 3D-printed parts let you find the assembly-order problems while the real stock is still on order.
5. **Off-season onboarding is what makes W1 productive.** They arrived at kickoff with new students who had already built a complete robot in a summer camp — which is why they could steal a working intake from a camp robot in Week 1. See §3.5 and §7.2.

### 2.7 Case study — a team exactly your size, at 4.5 hours a week

**[FACT]** FTC **17012 Precision Guessworks**, Lafayette, Indiana (Jefferson High School), from their public Open Alliance build thread (<https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/ftc-17012-precision-guessworks-2025-2026-build-thread/506118>), DECODE season. §2.6 is a large veteran programme; this one is the closer match to you. In their own words:

| Their stated conditions | Verbatim |
|---|---|
| Size | *"We anticipate having 4-8 students on the team this year."* |
| Meeting schedule | *"The team meets after school, 2:45 – 5:00pm twice a week following kickoff. This continues through December."* — **≈4.5 h/week**, roughly **one third** of the 12 h/week this document plans for |

| **Feb 6** (late region / LT) | 21 | Sep 20 | Oct 18 | Nov 15 | Dec 13 | Jan 24 | Feb 4 |

**Two things go wrong with a late first event [JUDGMENT]:**

| Failure | Mechanism | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope inflation** | Extra weeks get spent adding mechanisms, not perfecting one. The robot arrives in January less reliable than a November robot would have been. | Freeze scope at G2 anyway. Extra weeks are allocated **70% driver practice + reliability, 30% one additional capability**, and only if the scoring model ranks it above the reliability work. Write the split on the gate board. |
| **Holiday collapse** | Weeks of Dec 21 and Dec 28 are near-zero for most teams — school break, travel, closed facilities. A plan that assumes 17 continuous weeks actually has ~15. | Explicitly **zero out two December weeks** in the plan and pull G5 (robot alive) to **before Dec 20**. Then use whatever December time you do get as pure bonus. |

**Also true of a late event [FACT]:** you still read a Team Update every Thursday from Sep 17 and Q&A answers from Sep 28. A January team that ignored eleven Team Updates arrives with a robot built to the September rules.

**And the compounding one [FACT + JUDGMENT]:** advancement allocations are released **Nov 19, 2026**, and the last RCMP that feeds FIRST Championship is **Mar 21, 2027**. If your first entry-level event is in February, you have one, maybe two, of your three chronological shots before the RCMP window closes. Late-region teams do not get an easier season — they get **fewer attempts**, which raises the value of the dress rehearsal (G7) and of reliability over ceiling.

## 6. Iterate between events (W9 → February)

**[FACT]** The Overture 23619 pattern, run at three consecutive events, is the model. Their between-event process, as documented:

1. **Write the failure list at the event, not after.** Each entry: *Failure: <what happened>. Fix: <what was done, or "Unknown">.* They explicitly recorded **"Fix: Unknown"** twice for a recurring odometry problem — honest unknowns are more useful than invented causes, and judges reward them.
2. **Write the "What's next?" list before leaving the venue.** Theirs after event 1: retire the unused climber to save weight; change the intake and funnels; change camera settings; get rid of the structure rods.
3. **Execute the list between events, then re-verify on the field.**
4. **Root-cause the environment, not just the robot.** Their AprilTag failure was *sunlight at an outdoor venue*; the fix was a hardware swap (Arducam → Limelight 3A), not a software tweak.

**[JUDGMENT] The between-event loop, as a fixed 7-day cycle:**

| Day | Action |
|---|---|
| Event day, in the pit | Failure log entry within 5 minutes of every failure. One student owns the log. |
| Event day, drive home | "What's next?" list written and agreed, ranked by *matches lost per occurrence*. |
| Day +2 (first meeting) | Post-mortem: 45 minutes, whole team. Watch match video. Close the list into concrete tasks with owners. |
| Days +2 to +9 | Execute. **Rule: no new mechanisms between the first two events** — reliability first. New mechanisms only after event 2, and only if the scoring model says they are worth more than reliability. |
| Day +10 | Re-verify on the field: 6 consecutive full matches, no failure. |
| Every day | Driver practice. Between events is when driver skill compounds. |

**[JUDGMENT] Scouting between events.** Do not build the Overture-scale app (React capture → QR → Streamlit processing, a year of development). Do steal the workflow: **one scouter per robot per match, a lead scouter who validates data before it is committed, and a hard cutoff the day before the event on changing what you record.** For a 5-10 student team the right tool is a printed sheet plus a shared spreadsheet, backed by <https://ftcscout.org> for OPR and historical performance. Build the tool only if a student wants it as their own project.

**[FACT — checked 2026-08-22] ftcscout's public API does not have the BIOBUZZ season yet.** `https://api.ftcscout.org/rest/v1/teams/12736/events/2026` returns **HTTP 400, `Invalid season 2026`**, while the same path with `2025` returns full DECODE event and ranking data. **[JUDGMENT]** That is good news for the pre-season, not bad: **a student can build and test the entire scouting pipeline this month against 2025 data**, then change one number when the season opens. Building a data tool while the data is live and you also have matches to play is how scouting projects die. The same applies to the official FIRST API (`https://ftc-api.firstinspires.org/`, §1.7.2) — register the token now, not the night before your first event.

**Calendar reality check [FACT]:** in the live event data, the qualifier peak is the weeks of **Nov 30, Dec 7, Jan 4, Jan 11 and Jan 18** (34, 34, 22, 22 and 16 events respectively). League Tournaments cluster **late Jan through Feb**. Regional Championships run **Dec 5 – Mar 13** with the mass in **February**. Plan for **2-4 events between mid-November and late February**, with 3-6 weeks between each.

## 8. Meeting cadence — what a meeting actually contains

### 8.1 Roles for a 5-10 student team

**[JUDGMENT]** With 5-10 students every person carries ~1.5 roles and **every role needs a named backup.** Single-deep roles are the most common small-team failure.

| Role | Owns | Backup needed? |
|---|---|---|
| **Driver 1** | Robot controls, drive practice log | Yes — absolutely |
| **Driver 2 / Human Player** | Second controller, human-player tasks | Yes |
| **Drive Coach / Strategist** | Match strategy, alliance-selection input, scouting data → decisions | Yes |
| **Build lead** | Fabrication, assembly, spares inventory | Yes |
| **CAD lead** | Onshape master assembly, BOM, cut list | Yes — this is the most commonly single-deep role |
| **Software lead** | Repo, auto, teleop, tuning | Yes |
| **Software 2 / Vision** | AprilTags, sensors, telemetry | |
| **Documentation lead** | Build log, portfolio, build thread, photos | Yes |
| **Scouting lead** | Sheets, spreadsheet, pit scouting, ftcscout | |
| **Logistics** | Pit kit, batteries, tools, check-in, travel | |

**[JUDGMENT]** In a 6-student team: Driver 1 + Software 2; Driver 2 + Scouting; Coach + Strategy + Logistics; Build + Spares; CAD + Documentation; Software 1 + Vision. Every student attends every phase — **do not let the programmers skip prototyping or the builders skip the portfolio.** That specialization is what makes a small team brittle.

### 8.2 The 3-hour weekday meeting — standing agenda

```
0:00 – 0:10   STANDUP (10 min, standing, at the gate board)
              Each person, 45 seconds: did / doing / blocked.
              Coach reads the next gate date aloud. No problem-solving here.

0:10 – 0:20   RULES DELTA (Thursdays only; skip other days)
              Rules Night owner presents the Team Update diff + week's Q&A answers.
              One question: "does anything here change our robot, our auto, or our math?"

0:20 – 2:20   FOCUS BLOCK (2 hours, no meetings, phones in a box)
              At most TWO parallel workstreams. Named owner on each.
              Coach floats, unblocks, does not do the work.

2:20 – 2:40   FIELD BLOCK (20 min, mandatory once a robot exists)
              The robot touches the field EVERY meeting. Even if it is broken.
              Drive practice, or a measured test, or an auto run. Something on tiles.

2:40 – 2:52   LOG (12 min)
              Photos taken. Documentation lead writes one paragraph per workstream.
              Any new rule ambiguity goes on the Q&A candidates list.

2:52 – 3:00   CLOSE (8 min)
              Clean up. Each workstream names its single top task for next meeting.
              Written on the board. Meeting ends on time.
```

**[JUDGMENT] The two rules that make this work:** (1) **the robot touches the field every meeting** — a robot that lives on a table is a robot nobody can drive; (2) **the meeting ends on time.** Small teams burn out on 5-hour weeknights in October and have nothing left in January.

**[FACT] FIRST's own guidance disagrees with the length of this meeting** — the Mentor Manual says *"Sessions lasting 1-2 hours are the most productive"* (§2.0). **[JUDGMENT] Take the finding, not the prescription.** What decays after ~90 minutes is *undifferentiated focus*, not presence. The agenda above never asks for three hours of one mode: it is a 10-minute standing meeting, a 120-minute build block, a 20-minute physical field block, and 20 minutes of writing and closing — four different postures, with the mode change itself acting as the break. If you find hour three consistently produces nothing, the fix is not a shorter meeting; it is **moving the field block to the middle** as a genuine reset, or converting the last hour to a two-person shift (§2.4) so the rest of the team leaves at 2:00. **[FACT]** The same manual explicitly permits this: *"Depending on the role assignments, not every team member needs to be at every meeting."*

**A real counter-example to keep you honest [FACT]:** FTC 17012 runs **2:45–5:00 pm, twice a week** — 4.5 h/week, about a third of this plan — and still reached an archetype decision in Week 2 (§2.7). **[JUDGMENT]** If your team cannot sustain 12 h/week, do not fake it. Run their shape (two 2.25-hour meetings) and cut *scope* per §10.3 rather than running three-hour meetings that half the team attends.

### 8.3 The Saturday session (6-8 hours)

```
0:00 – 0:15   Standup + read the gate board + set the day's ONE objective
0:15 – 2:15   Focus block A — the hardest thing, while everyone is fresh
2:15 – 2:30   Break
2:30 – 4:00   Focus block B
4:00 – 4:45   LUNCH — eat together, no work. This is the team-building.
4:45 – 6:15   Focus block C  (in W6+, this becomes DRIVER PRACTICE, non-negotiable)
6:15 – 6:45   FULL-TEAM DEMO — every workstream shows the whole team what it built.
              This is the only reliable defence against two students building
              incompatible things for three weeks.
6:45 – 7:15   Log, photos, portfolio paragraphs, build-thread post
7:15 – 7:30   Clean up + plan next week's three meetings
```

### 8.4 What the meeting contains, by phase

| Phase | Weekday meeting | Saturday |
|---|---|---|
| **P0 Pre-season** | Drivetrain assembly; SDK setup; Skill Builders modules | Full build day + first drive practice |
| **P1 Kickoff** | — | See §4 (both days, full length) |
| **P2 Strategy** | Scoring-model refinement; rules reading; portfolio strategy pages | Prototype-and-measure day; whole-team strategy debate |
| **P3 Prototyping** | One mechanism per meeting, measured. CAD in parallel. | Two mechanisms in parallel + a measurement session; demo block matters most here |
| **P4 CAD/freeze** | CAD reviews with the whole team looking at the screen; BOM building | Full CAD day; **freeze review**: walk the assembly mechanism by mechanism against R102/R105/R503 |

### 8.5 The dress rehearsal (G7) — run it exactly like an event

**[JUDGMENT]** Four days before your first event, run a full simulated event day. This is the single highest-value 4-hour block in the pre-competition calendar and almost no small team does it.

| Simulated | Real requirement |
|---|---|
| Check-in with roster | **[FACT]** I102: an adult must check in **no later than 45 minutes before qualification matches start**, with at least one student present at the venue. |
| Self-inspection | Use the official inspection checklist. **[FACT]** The DECODE-season checklist and quick reference are already in `manuals/archive/supplemental/2025-26_DECODE_InspectionChecklist.pdf` and `..._InspectionQuickReference.pdf` — the R-rules they check are largely unchanged in BIOBUZZ V0. ### 8.6 Attendance math and the minimum-crew rule

**[JUDGMENT]** The single most under-modelled fact about a 5-10 student team is that **nobody attends everything.** Assume 80% attendance — normal for high-school students with jobs, sports, AP classes and college visits. On a 7-student team that is 5.6 students at an average meeting, and the distribution matters more than the mean: some meetings are 3.

Plan for it structurally rather than being surprised by it:

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Every workstream names a backup owner on kickoff Sunday.** No exceptions, including CAD. | The CAD lead's absence is the most expensive absence on a small team — it stops fabrication, BOM and freeze simultaneously. |
| **Minimum crew = 3.** Below 3, the meeting converts to a documented solo task, not a cancelled meeting. | Cancelling is how a team loses 9 hours in a 9-week season without noticing. |
| **Publish the "if only 3 show up, do this" list at the top of every meeting plan.** | Removes 20 minutes of milling around. Good defaults: driver practice, spares fabrication, portfolio pages, log backfill, battery maintenance. |
| **Never schedule the design-freeze review, the archetype decision or the dress rehearsal on a low-attendance date.** Check the school calendar in Week A and mark conflicts on the gate board. | These three are whole-team decisions; re-running them costs a week. |
| **Two drivers plus one trained backup.** | An illness the morning of an event should not cost you the season's ranking. |
| **[FACT]** I102 requires an adult to check in **no later than 45 minutes before qualification matches**, with at least one student present. | Adult coverage is a scheduling constraint, not a formality — confirm it per event when you register. |

**[JUDGMENT] The 10-minute rule that saves small teams:** the last 10 minutes of every meeting, the documentation lead writes what happened and each workstream names its single next task. A student who missed the meeting reads that entry and is productive on arrival. Without it, every absence costs the *team* a re-briefing, not just the absentee.

### 8.7 The async multiplier — the cheapest way to close the student-hour gap

**[FACT]** GM0 is explicit that meeting time is the wrong place to do work that does not need the room (<https://gm0.org/en/latest/docs/being-a-team/collaboration-and-efficiency.html>):

> *"Work from home. Team meetings should be used as time to catch up between squads, make decisions, and physically test parts of the robot. CAD design iterations can be created and reviewed at home, and software can be written outside of practice hours. This will allow you more time to test and reflect."*

**[FACT]** GM0 also sets the floor on meeting frequency, not the ceiling: *"it is recommended to schedule 1-2 mandatory meetings a week to facilitate collaboration"* and *"Host mandatory team meetings at least once a week, and share each squad's progress"* (<https://gm0.org/en/latest/docs/being-a-team/team-organization.html>).

**[JUDGMENT]** §10.4 puts the gap at roughly **4× in student-hours** against an elite programme. Async work is the only lever that adds hours instead of removing scope — and it is free. The rule that makes it safe: **anything that needs the field, the robot, or a decision happens in the room. Everything else can happen anywhere.**

| Must be in the room | Can be async | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Driver practice | CAD iteration | Onshape is cloud-native; review at the next meeting |
| Physical prototyping and testing | Code that does not need the robot — path planning, state machines, telemetry, unit-testable logic | Deploy at the meeting |
| Assembly, wiring, integration | Portfolio writing and layout | One owner, one document |
| Every gate decision (G1-G7) | Scouting spreadsheet and formula work | |
| Design-freeze review | Rules Night reading and the Team Update diff | Presented in 10 min at the next meeting |
| Full-team demo | Grant applications, sponsor letters, outreach scheduling | §1.8.1 |
| Judge-interview rehearsal | BOM building, ordering research, price checks | |

**The arithmetic [JUDGMENT].** Adding **3 async hours per student per week** to a 7-student team across the 9 weeks from kickoff to a Nov 14 event is **189 student-hours** on top of the ~800 in §10.4 — about **+24%**, for zero dollars and zero extra facility time. That is larger than every hardware compression in §10.4 combined.

**The three rules that stop async work from becoming divergence [JUDGMENT]:**

1. **It lands in the shared tool or it did not happen.** Onshape team document, the team GitHub, the shared drive. A CAD file on someone's laptop is not work; it is a liability. GM0's efficiency list says the same thing twice — *"Share"* and *"Don't hide problems."*
2. **Every async output gets 5 minutes at the next full-team demo** (§8.3). Unreviewed async work is exactly how two students spend three weeks building incompatible things.
3. **Async never covers for absence.** A student who misses meetings and works at home is not on the drive team, cannot pass the backup-owner drill (§3.5) and cannot answer a judge. Async is a multiplier on attendance, not a substitute for it.

## 10. Small-team compressions — and what each one costs

### 10.1 Safe compressions

**[JUDGMENT]** These reduce work without reducing the deliverable.

| Compression | How | What you give up |
|---|---|---|
| **Start from a StarterBot Base** | Build goBILDA's drop-center 6WD or mecanum variant, or the REV DUO channel drivetrain, as your actual competition chassis. Legal under R304 and R101 as long as **your team assembles it**. | ~2 weeks of drivetrain design. You give up a bespoke chassis footprint — rarely the thing that wins matches. |
| **One mechanism, perfected** | GM0's explicit advice: *"Perfect one objective first,"* and prioritize *consistency over scoring ability* (<https://gm0.org/en/latest/docs/design-skills/design-strategy.html>). | Versatility. In alliance selection, being the most reliable single-task robot is a strong pick. |
| **Buy the partial game set, DIY the perimeter** | §3.3 | ~$740 and some Saturday hours. No competitive cost. |
| **Skip custom scouting software** | Paper + spreadsheet + ftcscout.org | A student project. No competitive cost at your first 2 events. |
| **Reuse last year's software architecture** | R304 explicitly permits it | Nothing. Do this. |
| **Design freeze one week earlier (Oct 11)** | Accept a simpler mechanism | Peak scoring ceiling. Buys a week of driver practice — usually a net win for a small team. |
| **Half practice field** | 6 ft × 12 ft of tiles | Full-field auto tuning and realistic driving. Acceptable up to event 1; not acceptable by January. |

### 10.2 Dangerous compressions and their failure modes

| Compression | Immediate appeal | **Failure mode** | Early warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cut driver practice** | "The robot isn't done, so we can't practice" | **THE classic**, and GM0 names it as such: *"A persistent problem with new teams is neglecting driver practice. Drive practice is to be done throughout the season, **not the week before competition**"* — and the elite yardstick, *"By April's world championships, most top teams have run hundreds of practice matches"* (<https://gm0.org/en/latest/docs/design-skills/design-strategy.html>). A robot that is 20% better but driven by someone with 3 hours of stick time loses to a simpler robot with 30 hours. Cycle time is dominated by driver skill, not mechanism speed. Practice hours cannot be recovered — they can only be spent before the event, at a rate of roughly 8-12 full matches a week (§2.4). | It is Week 8 and no student has 10 logged hours of driving |
| **Serialize CAD after prototyping** | "We can't CAD until we know the mechanism" | Design freeze slips 2 weeks → parts arrive late → integration compresses into event week → nothing is tuned. Drivetrain and electrical CAD **never** needed to wait. | CAD workstream is idle in W1-W2 |
| **Start programming after the build** | "There's nothing to program yet" | Autonomous never works. Auto is where matches are won and it needs the most iterations against real hardware. | No auto path has ever run on tiles by Nov 1 |
| **Write the portfolio in the last week** | "We'll do it when we know what we built" | The photos and data do not exist. You cannot retroactively document a prototype you threw away in September. **[FACT]** A201.E limits you to work since Jan 1, 2026 — but only work you actually recorded. | No dated build-log entries for W2-W5 |
| **Skip the mock field elements at kickoff** | "The game set ships Monday" | Your entire kickoff weekend's prototyping is untethered from real geometry, and you re-do it in W2 against the real element. | Sunday's prototypes were tested against nothing |
| **Skip the dress rehearsal** | "We'll be fine, it's just a qualifier" | Preventable event-day surprises: failing inspection on a robot-sign rule, a battery you did not charge, a portfolio nobody printed. Half of a small team's first-event losses are logistics, not robot. | G7 is not on the board |
| **One student per role, no backups** | "There aren't enough of us" | One illness, one college visit, one family trip stops an entire workstream for a week. In a 9-week season that is 11% of the calendar. | The CAD lead is the only person with an Onshape login |
| **Attempt every scoring task** | "More points is better" | 8 motors and 8 servos (R503) and an 18-inch cube (R102) are hard limits. Four half-working mechanisms score less than two working ones and take three times as long to debug. | Your mechanism list grew after G2 |
| **Rebuild V2 in January without a plan** | "We learned so much" | **[FACT]** GM0 notes rookies should expect *"50-100+ hours to rebuild a robot from the ground up"* and discourages mid-season rebuilds for inexperienced teams. A January rebuild with 9 hours/week is 6-11 weeks — past your regional championship. | The rebuild has no gate dates |
| **No spares** | "We'll be careful" | One 3/8 in. shaft bends (as happened to Overture 23619 with 7075-T6 aluminum) and your event ends. | Spares inventory is empty at G6 |
| **One tool, no fallback** | "We have a 3D printer / router, we're fine" | **[FACT]** FTC 12736 lost their CNC router in stages through October — chuck, then spindle, then the controller (*"aluminum got into the computer and fried the whole thing"*) — with replacement grant money weeks away. Their season only survived because they **rebuilt the entire robot in wood** and planned a polycarb rebuild later (§2.6). A team with one manufacturing method and no plan B loses weeks, not days. | You cannot name, in writing, what you would do tomorrow if the printer died |
| **Budgeting on money you have been awarded** | "The grant came through" | Awarded ≠ available. FTC 12736's city router grant was awarded and then unavailable *"for another couple weeks."* Meanwhile the grant windows themselves close **Sep 30 – Oct 31**, inside your prototyping phase (§1.8.1). | A G4 parts order depends on funds not yet in the account |

### 10.3 The serialize/parallel decision rule

**[JUDGMENT]** When you must cut, cut in this order:

1. **Cut robot scope first** — one fewer mechanism.
2. **Cut fabrication ambition second** — simpler brackets, COTS structure over custom plates.
3. **Cut event count third** — two well-prepared events beat four scrambled ones.
4. **Cut documentation polish fourth** — a plain, honest 15 pages beats a beautiful 15 pages written in a panic. Never cut the *log*, only the *design*.
5. **Never cut driver practice, the dress rehearsal, or Rules Night.** These are cheap, they are hours not dollars, and they are the three things that most reliably separate a small team that punches above its weight from one that does not.

### 10.4 The hours arithmetic — where the deficit actually lands

**[JUDGMENT]** Compressions are usually argued qualitatively. Do the arithmetic instead; it tells you exactly how much you must cut.

Kickoff (Sep 12) to a Nov 14 first event is **9 weeks**, plus the kickoff weekend itself.

| | Small team (this team) | Elite comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Meeting load | 2 × 3 h weekday + 1 × 6 h Saturday = **12 h/week** | **~20 h/week** — **[FACT]** FTC 23513 I.N.T. Robotics stated *"We plan to meet roughly 20 hours per week in person"* (<https://www.chiefdelphi.com/t/ftc-23513-i-n-t-robotics-open-alliance-build-thread-2025-2026-season/506299>) |
| 9 weeks of meetings | 108 h | 180 h |
| Kickoff weekend | +20 h | +20 h |
| **Team-hours before event 1** | **~128 h** | **~200 h** |
| Students | 6-7 | 15-25 |
| **Student-hours before event 1** | **~800** | **~3,500** |

**[FACT] Calibrate both columns against FIRST's own published expectation** (§2.0, Mentor Manual): minimum **26 h**, average **37 h**, *"the more intense end"* **180 h per student per season**. This plan's ~115 student-hours to event 1 (≈800 ÷ 7) grows to roughly **250-300 by April** — i.e. **~1.5× what FIRST calls intense**, and about **7× the program average**. The elite comparison column is **~3× FIRST's intense end**, in-person, before async. **[JUDGMENT] Two uses for that.** First, it is the honest number to put in front of parents and students in Week A. Second, it re-frames what "competitive" costs: a team at FIRST's *average* 37 hours is not under-committed, it is simply playing a different game, and the distance between them and a regional-championship team is a factor of seven in hours — not a factor of seven in talent.

**The real gap is not 1.6× in team-hours — it is ~4× in student-hours.** You cannot close that by working faster or later; you close it by **doing fewer things**, and by putting AI on the work that is not hands-on.

**[JUDGMENT] Where the ~72 team-hours and the parallelism deficit come back — estimates, calibrate against your own team:**

| Lever | Team-hours saved | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Start from a vendor **StarterBot Base** instead of designing a drivetrain (legal under R304/R101 if your students assemble it) | **25-40 h** | A generic chassis footprint. Rarely decisive. |
| **Reuse last season's software architecture** (R304 explicitly permits it) | **20-30 h** | Nothing. Do this unconditionally. |
| **One perfected mechanism** instead of three half-working ones (GM0: *"Perfect one objective first"*) | **30-50 h** | Scoring ceiling. Buys reliability, which is what alliance captains actually pick. |
| **COTS structure** (channel, brackets, gearboxes) over custom plates | **15-25 h** | Weight and elegance. Not points. |
| **AI on rules digests, log-to-portfolio drafting, scouting math, boilerplate OpModes** (§9) | **10-15 h** | Nothing, *if* students still make every decision. |
| **DIY perimeter, partial game set** (§3.3) | ~$740 and 4 h of Saturday | No competitive cost. |
| **Async work — 3 h/student/week outside meetings** on CAD, code, portfolio, scouting math and grant applications (§8.7) | **+189 student-hours** over 9 weeks on a 7-student team (~+24%) | Nothing, *if* it lands in the shared tool and gets 5 minutes at the full-team demo. This is the only lever that **adds** hours instead of cutting scope. |
| **Total recovered** | **~100-160 team-hours, plus ~190 async student-hours** | |

**The point of the table [JUDGMENT]:** the cuts above roughly cover the deficit — but only if you take them **before** you need them. Every one of them is a decision available in September and unavailable in November. A small team that takes none of them in September takes all of them in November, under duress, badly, and skips driver practice to do it.
